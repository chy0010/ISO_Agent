# Transmission Congestion and Interchange

## What congestion is

Transmission congestion occurs when a transmission line or piece of equipment
reaches its physical limit and cannot carry any more power. When this happens,
the grid operator cannot always dispatch the cheapest available generation,
because delivering that power would overload the constrained line. Instead, more
expensive generation on the "downstream" side of the constraint must run to
serve local demand.

## How congestion shows up in prices

Congestion is the main reason LMPs differ across locations. The congestion
component of the LMP captures this: locations on the constrained (downstream)
side see higher prices, while locations with surplus cheap generation on the
other side see lower prices. If you observe two nearby locations with very
different prices, a binding transmission constraint between them is the usual
cause. When there is no congestion anywhere, prices across the grid converge
toward a single system-wide energy price.

## What drives congestion

Congestion is driven by the combination of where generation is located, where
demand is, and the capacity of the lines connecting them. High wind output in a
remote area with limited transmission can cause congestion, because the cheap
wind power cannot all be exported. Heavy demand in a load pocket with limited
import capacity can also cause it. Congestion patterns shift throughout the day
as demand and generation change.

## Interchange between grids

Neighboring grid operators exchange power across their borders; this is called
interchange. A grid importing power is drawing on generation from a neighbor;
a grid exporting is sending surplus generation out. Interchange helps balance
supply and demand efficiently across a wider area and is one reason a region's
generation may not exactly match its own load — the difference is made up by
imports or exports.
